# Sources

Every "Why it works" bullet in a layout spec, and every line of [why-it-works.md](./why-it-works.md), cites one or more sources from this list by its short name (for example Treisman & Gelade 1980). Each entry says what the source is cited for. A source supports that claim and no more: cite it for nothing else.

Each entry was checked against a published record (journal, publisher or standards body) on 2026-10-21. A source that could not be checked was left out.

## Attention and seeing

<a id="itti-koch-2001"></a>
**Itti & Koch 2001.** Itti, L., & Koch, C. (2001). Computational modelling of visual attention. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 2(3), 194–203.
Cited for: attention goes first to the region that differs most from its surroundings in size, contrast or colour, and two equally strong regions compete for it.

<a id="treisman-gelade-1980"></a>
**Treisman & Gelade 1980.** Treisman, A. M., & Gelade, G. (1980). A feature-integration theory of attention. *Cognitive Psychology*, 12(1), 97–136.
Cited for: one item that differs from the rest by a single feature, such as colour, is found at once, whatever else is on the page.

<a id="von-restorff-1933"></a>
**von Restorff 1933.** von Restorff, H. (1933). Über die Wirkung von Bereichsbildungen im Spurenfeld. *Psychologische Forschung*, 18, 299–342. Read with Hunt, R. R. (1995). The subtlety of distinctiveness: What von Restorff really did. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 2(1), 105–112.
Cited for: an item set apart from the items around it is remembered better than they are. The effect needs the other items to be alike.

<a id="potter-2014"></a>
**Potter et al. 2014.** Potter, M. C., Wyble, B., Hagmann, C. E., & McCourt, E. S. (2014). Detecting meaning in RSVP at 13 ms per picture. *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics*, 76(2), 270–279.
Cited for: people take the gist of an image from a very brief glance, well under a second.

<a id="lindgaard-2006"></a>
**Lindgaard et al. 2006.** Lindgaard, G., Fernandes, G., Dudek, C., & Brown, J. (2006). Attention web designers: You have 50 milliseconds to make a good first impression! *Behaviour & Information Technology*, 25(2), 115–126.
Cited for: people judge the visual appeal of a page in about 50 ms, and that first judgement matches the one they make with more time.

<a id="pieters-wedel-2004"></a>
**Pieters & Wedel 2004.** Pieters, R., & Wedel, M. (2004). Attention capture and transfer in advertising: Brand, pictorial, and text-size effects. *Journal of Marketing*, 68(2), 36–50.
Cited for: in print ads, the attention a text element gets grows with its surface size, and attention to the brand element helps people remember the brand.

<a id="wcag-22"></a>
**W3C 2023 (WCAG 2.2).** World Wide Web Consortium. (2023). *Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2*. Success criteria 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) and 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast. https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/
Cited for: text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background, large text at least 3:1. WCAG counts 24px (18pt) as large; the `contrast` check waits until 36px, which is stricter.

## Grouping and figure–ground

<a id="wertheimer-1923"></a>
**Wertheimer 1923.** Wertheimer, M. (1923). Untersuchungen zur Lehre von der Gestalt II. *Psychologische Forschung*, 4, 301–350.
Cited for: things that sit close together (proximity), look alike (similarity) or line up along one path (good continuation) are seen as one group.

<a id="palmer-1992"></a>
**Palmer 1992.** Palmer, S. E. (1992). Common region: A new principle of perceptual grouping. *Cognitive Psychology*, 24(3), 436–447.
Cited for: things inside one bounded area, such as a panel, a card or a filled shape, are seen as one group, even against proximity.

<a id="wagemans-2012"></a>
**Wagemans et al. 2012.** Wagemans, J., Elder, J. H., Kubovy, M., Palmer, S. E., Peterson, M. A., Singh, M., & von der Heydt, R. (2012). A century of Gestalt psychology in visual perception: I. Perceptual grouping and figure–ground organization. *Psychological Bulletin*, 138(6), 1172–1217.
Cited for: the current review of grouping and figure–ground; the grouping principles above hold in modern experiments.

## Reading and type

<a id="legge-bigelow-2011"></a>
**Legge & Bigelow 2011.** Legge, G. E., & Bigelow, C. A. (2011). Does print size matter for reading? A review of findings from vision science and typography. *Journal of Vision*, 11(5):8.
Cited for: reading slows sharply below a critical print size; above it, speed holds steady across a wide range of sizes.

<a id="dyson-2004"></a>
**Dyson 2004.** Dyson, M. C. (2004). How physical text layout affects reading from screen. *Behaviour & Information Technology*, 23(6), 377–393.
Cited for: line length and line spacing change how fast and how well text is read on screen.

<a id="tinker-1963"></a>
**Tinker 1963.** Tinker, M. A. (1963). *Legibility of Print*. Iowa State University Press.
Cited for: running text set in capitals is read more slowly than the same text in lower case.

<a id="nielsen-2006"></a>
**Nielsen 2006.** Nielsen, J. (2006). F-shaped pattern for reading web content. Nielsen Norman Group. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/
Cited for: on screen, people scan more than they read: they look at the start of lines and the first words, down the left edge.

<a id="lorch-1989"></a>
**Lorch 1989.** Lorch, R. F. (1989). Text-signaling devices and their effects on reading and memory processes. *Educational Psychology Review*, 1(3), 209–234.
Cited for: headings, numbers and other signals that show a text's structure help readers find their way and remember the structure.

<a id="yang-2012"></a>
**Yang 2012.** Yang, S. S. (2012). Eye movements on restaurant menus: A revisitation on gaze motion and consumer scanpaths. *International Journal of Hospitality Management*, 31(3), 1021–1029.
Cited for: diners read a menu in order, top to bottom like a book, and found no single "sweet spot" on the page.

## Memory and thinking

<a id="miller-1956"></a>
**Miller 1956.** Miller, G. A. (1956). The magical number seven, plus or minus two: Some limits on our capacity for processing information. *Psychological Review*, 63(2), 81–97.
Cited for: people hold only a few items in mind at once, and grouping items into chunks raises how much they hold.

<a id="cowan-2001"></a>
**Cowan 2001.** Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 24(1), 87–114.
Cited for: the working limit is nearer three to five chunks than seven.

<a id="sweller-1988"></a>
**Sweller 1988.** Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning. *Cognitive Science*, 12(2), 257–285.
Cited for: working memory is limited, and material that makes people hold or search for extra things leaves less room to understand the point.

<a id="mayer-2009"></a>
**Mayer 2009.** Mayer, R. E. (2009). *Multimedia Learning* (2nd ed.). Cambridge University Press.
Cited for: four of its principles, each tested in experiments: coherence (people learn more when extra words and pictures are left out), signaling (cues that show the structure help), segmenting (material in user-paced parts helps), spatial contiguity (related words and pictures placed together help).

<a id="murdock-1962"></a>
**Murdock 1962.** Murdock, B. B. (1962). The serial position effect of free recall. *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 64(5), 482–488.
Cited for: people remember the first and last items of a sequence best.

<a id="ausubel-1960"></a>
**Ausubel 1960.** Ausubel, D. P. (1960). The use of advance organizers in the learning and retention of meaningful verbal material. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 51(5), 267–272.
Cited for: a short overview given before the material helps people learn and keep it.

<a id="zacks-swallow-2007"></a>
**Zacks & Swallow 2007.** Zacks, J. M., & Swallow, K. M. (2007). Event segmentation. *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 16(2), 80–84.
Cited for: people split a continuous experience into parts at clear boundaries, and those boundaries help them remember it.

<a id="hick-1952"></a>
**Hick 1952.** Hick, W. E. (1952). On the rate of gain of information. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 4(1), 11–26.
Cited for: the time to choose grows with the number of options.

<a id="iyengar-lepper-2000"></a>
**Iyengar & Lepper 2000.** Iyengar, S. S., & Lepper, M. R. (2000). When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing? *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 79(6), 995–1006.
Cited for: in their studies, fewer options led more people to act (to buy, to write an essay) than many options did.

<a id="loewenstein-1994"></a>
**Loewenstein 1994.** Loewenstein, G. (1994). The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation. *Psychological Bulletin*, 116(1), 75–98.
Cited for: curiosity rises when people notice a specific gap in what they know, and it moves them to close it.

<a id="gollwitzer-1999"></a>
**Gollwitzer 1999.** Gollwitzer, P. M. (1999). Implementation intentions: Strong effects of simple plans. *American Psychologist*, 54(7), 493–503.
Cited for: people who know when and where they will act are much more likely to act.

<a id="gentner-markman-1997"></a>
**Gentner & Markman 1997.** Gentner, D., & Markman, A. B. (1997). Structure mapping in analogy and similarity. *American Psychologist*, 52(1), 45–56.
Cited for: when two things share one structure, people line them up part by part and notice the differences that sit in matching places.

## Fluency and trust

<a id="reber-2004"></a>
**Reber et al. 2004.** Reber, R., Schwarz, N., & Winkielman, P. (2004). Processing fluency and aesthetic pleasure: Is beauty in the perceiver's processing experience? *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 8(4), 364–382.
Cited for: things that are easy to perceive (clear figure–ground, high contrast, symmetry, simplicity) are liked more.

<a id="reber-schwarz-1999"></a>
**Reber & Schwarz 1999.** Reber, R., & Schwarz, N. (1999). Effects of perceptual fluency on judgments of truth. *Consciousness and Cognition*, 8(3), 338–342.
Cited for: the same statement shown in a colour that is easier to read against its background was judged true more often.

<a id="hovland-weiss-1951"></a>
**Hovland & Weiss 1951.** Hovland, C. I., & Weiss, W. (1951). The influence of source credibility on communication effectiveness. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 15(4), 635–650.
Cited for: the same message changes more minds when it comes from a source people trust.

<a id="cialdini-2021"></a>
**Cialdini 2021.** Cialdini, R. B. (2021). *Influence, New and Expanded: The Psychology of Persuasion*. Harper Business.
Cited for: three of its principles: social proof (people follow what others like them do and say), authority, and scarcity (things in short supply or for a short time are wanted more).

<a id="worchel-1975"></a>
**Worchel et al. 1975.** Worchel, S., Lee, J., & Adewole, A. (1975). Effects of supply and demand on ratings of object value. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 32(5), 906–914.
Cited for: the same item was rated more desirable when there were fewer of it.

<a id="inman-mcalister-1994"></a>
**Inman & McAlister 1994.** Inman, J. J., & McAlister, L. (1994). Do coupon expiration dates affect consumer behavior? *Journal of Marketing Research*, 31(3), 423–428.
Cited for: coupon use rises as the expiry date comes near; a stated end date prompts action.

## Brand

<a id="zajonc-1968"></a>
**Zajonc 1968.** Zajonc, R. B. (1968). Attitudinal effects of mere exposure. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 9(2, Pt. 2), 1–27.
Cited for: people come to like things more the more often they see them.

<a id="keller-1993"></a>
**Keller 1993.** Keller, K. L. (1993). Conceptualizing, measuring, and managing customer-based brand equity. *Journal of Marketing*, 57(1), 1–22.
Cited for: brand awareness (recognising and recalling the brand) is the base of brand equity, built by repeated exposure to the brand's elements.

<a id="romaniuk-2018"></a>
**Romaniuk 2018.** Romaniuk, J. (2018). *Building Distinctive Brand Assets*. Oxford University Press.
Cited for: a colour, logo or shape becomes a brand asset only when people link it to the brand, and that link is built by using it the same way, often, over time.

<a id="simonin-ruth-1998"></a>
**Simonin & Ruth 1998.** Simonin, B. L., & Ruth, J. A. (1998). Is a company known by the company it keeps? Assessing the spillover effects of brand alliances on consumer brand attitudes. *Journal of Marketing Research*, 35(1), 30–42.
Cited for: how people feel about a brand alliance carries over to how they feel about each partner brand.

## Products in a scene

<a id="macinnis-price-1987"></a>
**MacInnis & Price 1987.** MacInnis, D. J., & Price, L. L. (1987). The role of imagery in information processing: Review and extensions. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 13(4), 473–491.
Cited for: pictures that help people imagine using a product shape how they judge it.

<a id="elder-krishna-2012"></a>
**Elder & Krishna 2012.** Elder, R. S., & Krishna, A. (2012). The "visual depiction effect" in advertising: Facilitating embodied mental simulation through product orientation. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 38(6), 988–1003.
Cited for: a product shown ready for the viewer's hand helps them picture using it and raised purchase intention in their studies.

<a id="peck-shu-2009"></a>
**Peck & Shu 2009.** Peck, J., & Shu, S. B. (2009). The effect of mere touch on perceived ownership. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 36(3), 434–447.
Cited for: touching an object, and in their studies imagining touching it, raises the feeling that it is one's own.

## Charts

<a id="cleveland-mcgill-1984"></a>
**Cleveland & McGill 1984.** Cleveland, W. S., & McGill, R. (1984). Graphical perception: Theory, experimentation, and application to the development of graphical methods. *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, 79(387), 531–554.
Cited for: people compare values most accurately as positions along one common scale, such as bar ends against one baseline.

<a id="pandey-2015"></a>
**Pandey et al. 2015.** Pandey, A. V., Rall, K., Satterthwaite, M. L., Nov, O., & Bertini, E. (2015). How deceptive are deceptive visualizations? An empirical analysis of common distortion techniques. *Proceedings of CHI 2015*, 1469–1478.
Cited for: a bar chart with a cut axis made readers judge differences as larger than they are.

<a id="tufte-2001"></a>
**Tufte 2001.** Tufte, E. R. (2001). *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (2nd ed.). Graphics Press.
Cited for: the size of a graphic's marks should be in proportion to the numbers they stand for, and ink that shows no data should be cut.

## Slides

<a id="alley-neeley-2005"></a>
**Alley & Neeley 2005.** Alley, M., & Neeley, K. A. (2005). Rethinking the design of presentation slides: A case for sentence headlines and visual evidence. *Technical Communication*, 52(4), 417–426.
Cited for: a slide headed by one full-sentence claim, backed by visual evidence, states its point more clearly than a topic phrase over bullets.

<a id="garner-alley-2013"></a>
**Garner & Alley 2013.** Garner, J. K., & Alley, M. (2013). How the design of presentation slides affects audience comprehension: A case for the assertion–evidence approach. *International Journal of Engineering Education*, 29(6), 1564–1579.
Cited for: audiences shown sentence-headline slides with visual evidence understood the content better than audiences shown topic-and-bullet slides.

<a id="duarte-2008"></a>
**Duarte 2008.** Duarte, N. (2008). *slide:ology: The Art and Science of Creating Great Presentations*. O'Reilly Media.
Cited for: practitioner guidance: a slide the audience can take in within a few seconds keeps them listening to the speaker.

## Ads on the web

<a id="benway-1998"></a>
**Benway 1998.** Benway, J. P. (1998). Banner blindness: The irony of attention grabbing on the World Wide Web. *Proceedings of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society 42nd Annual Meeting*, 463–467.
Cited for: people looking for something on a page often miss items that look like banner ads, even large bright ones.

<a id="burke-2005"></a>
**Burke et al. 2005.** Burke, M., Hornof, A., Nilsen, E., & Gorman, N. (2005). High-cost banner blindness: Ads increase perceived workload, hinder visual search, and are forgotten. *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction*, 12(4), 423–445.
Cited for: animated ads slowed people's search and added to their sense of workload, and the ads themselves were poorly remembered.

## Never do

- Never cite a source that is not on this list. Add it here first, with the one claim it supports, after checking it against a published record.
- Never cite a source for more than its "Cited for" line.
